In 1948, Margaret Willis was born in Akron, Ohio, and grew up in the nearby city of Cuyahoga Falls.

She attended Kent State University and was in her senior year in 1970, when National Guard troops fired on students protesting the Vietnam War.

In 1971, Margaret married Tom Morton, her high school boyfriend, who enlisted in the Navy after attending Dartmouth College. The newlyweds spent the first years of their marriage in Gaetano, Italy, where Tom was stationed.

Morton, Chapel Street studio, New Haven, 1986 In 1975, Margaret and Tom moved to New Haven, where Margaret earned a master’s degree in graphic design and Tom in architecture. Margaret sought commercial assignments while teaching and painting. She had a studio in the offices of renowned architect Cesar Pelli, where Tom worked. Trigon, oil on canvas, 1988 Ombra Luce, pastel, 1988 Margaret’s art was inspired by her Italian experience. She developed two bodies of work, both monochrome: a series of large female heads based on Italian Renaissance painting, and shadow paintings, abstractions informed by architecture. PHOTO: Cooper Union (photo Xuemeng Zhang) PHOTO: Art of the Book postcard In the early 1980s, Margaret began teaching at Cooper Union in New York City while living in New Haven. In 1985, Cooper offered her a tenure track position in graphic design, and the couple moved to New York. Promoted to full professor in 1999, Margaret taught at Cooper Union until she retired in 2018. Her 2-Dimensional Design course was a foundation class for all first-year art students, and her Art of the Book class, accompanied by an annual exhibition, was legendary. 311 East 10th Street, East Village (photo Xuemeng Zhang) Margaret and Tom bought an apartment in a tenement on the north side of Tompkins Square Park in the East Village, a few blocks from Cooper Union. During their first years there, a “tent city” built by unhoused people grew inside the park, which became the epicenter of the city’s growing homelessness crisis. In the wake of a riot between police and protesters in 1988, Margaret took up the camera to photograph the encampment. Her longstanding interest in architecture initially drew her to document the structures assembled from objects found on the streets. To photograph these structures, she asked permission of their owners and established relationships with them. In 1991, when the park was closed for a two-year renovation, she followed many park residents to nearby vacant lots, which in turn led her to other homeless communities throughout Manhattan. By photographing these structures and getting to know their owners, Margaret came to consider them not just tents but homes with gardens. Nathaniel’s garden, Tompkins Square Park, 1991 Nathaniel was known as the Mayor of Tompkin’s Square Park. His tent and garden were destroyed in 14-degree weather on December 14, 1989. He returned to the park and built another home – pictured here – which was destroyed on June 3, 1991, when the park closed for renovation. Jimmy’s Garden, 1991 Margaret’s two photographs of Jimmy’s garden dramatically capture the essentials of her project. Jimmy built a pond in the center of a vacant lot, separate from his tent, which can be seen in the back of the lot against a building. To construct the pond, he assembled stones, garbage bags, five tires, an upholstered armchair, a skid, a refrigerator shelf, a wooden fence and a metal post. He filled the pond with water carried from a fire hydrant down the street and purchased goldfish to inhabit it. Site of Jimmy’s Garden, 1991 Eight days later, when Margaret returned to photograph Jimmy’s garden again, the lot had been bulldozed, and Jimmy had disappeared. Margaret Morton, 1994 (courtesy Fred Conrad) The impermanence of these dwellings and the desire to document them created a sense of urgency in Margaret. The idea of a book began to form, which she initially called, “The Architecture of Despair.” Wishing to allow those she photographed to tell their own stories, she recorded their conversations and asked them to sign releases for the use of their image and words. In the mid-1990s, in addition to teaching and working tirelessly in the field, she produced her first two books. In 1993, she and Diana Balmori co-authored Transitory Gardens, Uprooted Lives; and in 1995, she published The Tunnel. Although Tom and Margaret separated at this time for reasons unrelated to her work, the person she was becoming was very different from the Navy wife Tom married in 1971. Transitory Gardens, Uprooted Lives, Yale University Press, 1993 Margaret’s first book was a collaboration with Diana Balmori, a landscape architect who was her friend and mentor. In the text, Balmori placed the gardens of New York City’s unhoused inhabitants in the context of garden history: “In the reuse of nearly everything discarded, the sparing use of water, and the economical treatment of space, these gardens speak the language of our time. . . . To us, these gardens seem a testimony to the essential need for a garden: perhaps through them, we can learn again what a garden is.” The Tunnel, Yale University Press, 1995 In 1991, Margaret learned of a community living in an abandoned Amtrak tunnel on Manhattan’s west side. She returned to the tunnel regularly between 1991 and 1995 when the residents were forced out, and Amtrak reopened the tunnel to train traffic. Subsequently,Margaret helped the Coalition for the Homeless place many tunnel residents in supportive housing. Bernard, 1994 With The Tunnel, Margaret established the template for her subsequent books. The photographs are printed full-page, and the text, except for Margaret’s brief, factual introduction, are first-person accounts. Accompanying this photograph of Bernard, who was her guide to the tunnel and its community, are his reflections on living in the tunnel: “This existence has done so much for me. It’s taken me from my vanity. . . I had lived on the West Side for twelve years, and for me to be in my neighborhood and just to adapt to the lifestyle of a homeless person. . . It’s been remarkable that I have done it without shame.” Fragile Dwelling, Aperture Foundation, 2000 By 1997, all the encampments in Manhattan were cleared, signaling the end of Margaret’s field work. In the next few years, she focused on exhibitions and preparing the book she had originally envisioned that summarized the entire project. Renamed Fragile Dwelling, it presents an inventory of communities, beginning with those on the Lower East Side, which were featured in Transitory Gardens, and The Hill at the foot of the Manhattan Bridge, as well as encampments along the East and Hudson Rivers. She was now able to see her subject in its entirety: the 1970s fiscal crisis; the chronic rise of homelessness; city government’s alliance with real estate interests to promote gentrification; and the harsh crackdown of the Guiliani administration, which pushed homeless people out of the security of their communities into the shadows. Toby and Calli in the kitchen, 1994 Glass House was an abandoned glass factory a few blocks from Margaret’s apartment, which a group of squatters were renovating. In 1993, Margaret earned their trust and documented their community in photographs, video and audio recordings, but her work was cut short after only four months, when the police stormed the building and evicted the residents. Glass House, Penn State University Press, 2004 Years later, Margaret received a letter from one of the Glass House residents, which led her to track down the others as best she could. This led to her to publish one more book on New York’s homelessness crisis of the 1990s. Untitled [page 57] In the summer of 2002, Margaret accompanied her friend Virlana Tkacz, Director of the Yara Arts Group, to document sites referenced in a Kyrgyz epic poem that Virlana planned to adapt as a theater production. On their six-week journey into the mountains of Kyrgyzstan, Margaret encountered ancestral Islamic cemeteries that astonished her. Cities of the Dead, The Ancestral Cemeteries of Kyrgyzstan, University of Washington Press, 2014 On several trips in the next three years, she documented these miniature cities (the architectural forms are half-human scale), which culminated in Cities of the Dead. This was Margaret’s first project shot with a digital camera.
