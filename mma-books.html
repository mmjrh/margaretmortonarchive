Transitory Gardens, Uprooted Lives

With text by Morton's colleague at Yale University, landscape architect Diana Balmori, Transitory Gardens, Uprooted Lives addresses gardens built by New York City unhoused people. Gardening despite their economic circumstances, the subjects of this book are aware that their gardens await imminent destruction by city authorities and the private owners of their appropriated spaces. The images that make up this book span a wide swath of the Lower East Side, dispute "the garden as the domain of settled or prosperous individuals only," and consider how such spaces might reflect their caretakers' desire for basic comforts.

The Tunnel

The subjects of The Tunnel have built homes in an Amtrak tunnel under the West Side Highway, which some of them refer to as the "Freedom Tunnel". Morton collected oral histories and shot photographs there between 1991 and 1995, witnessing the accommodations residents had improvised from cinder-block structures and elevated recesses in the tunnel walls. Tunnel residents speak to their individual circumstances and emphasize the modest privacy and dignity that the Tunnel affords, especially in comparison to the inadequacy of city shelters. When Amtrak announced its intention to evict these residents in 1994, Morton and filmmaker Marc Singer worked with Coalition for the Homeless to offer those willing permanent housing.

Fragile Dwelling

Fragile Dwelling’s photographs and oral histories (1990-1996) consider the aesthetic and architectural sensibilities of unhoused peoples in downtown New York City. The book records sites where its subjects built homes until their demolition by the Dinkins and Giuliani administrations: Bushville, (East 4th St. and Avenue D), The Hill (at the base of the Manhattan Bridge in Chinatown), and along the East River before its transformation into a public greenway. Morton’s interviews detail the methods of builders making homes from warehouse pallets, junked auto parts, and demolition scrap. As historian Alan Trachtenberg writes in his introduction, the book refuses a binary choice between “art or reform, beauty or anger, pleasure or discontent," and looks instead to understand its builders' ingenuity along with the broader circumstances of their abjection.

Glass House

The photographs and oral histories in Glass House document the resilience of thirty-five squatters, many still in their teens, who occupied a derelict glass factory on New York City’s Lower East Side. Ranging in age from teens to middle age, their efforts to rehabilitate the building involved clearing debris, hammering through concrete-covered window openings, repairing rotten joists, replacing much of the roof, and constructing rooms with salvaged building materials. After living there for sixteen months, the squatters were evicted from Glass House in winter 1994. Offering insights into the era’s housing struggles, Glass House is entangled with the broader socio-economic history of New York: the city’s turn to austerity in the mid 1970s, its disinvestment from low-income neighborhoods in the 1980s, and the disastrous affordability crisis it faces today.

In 2025, Glass House was the subject of an exhibition at Interference Archive, a volunteer-run archive for social movements in Brooklyn, New York.

Cities of the Dead: The Ancestral Cemeteries of Kyrgyzstan

Cities of the Dead emerged from Morton's 2006 travels in Kyrgyzstan with her friend Virlana Tkacz, founding director of the Yara Arts Group, a resident company at La MaMa Experimental Theatre Club in New York. Captivated by Kyrgyz ancestral cemeteries she saw on that trip, Morton made return visits to shoot digital photographs at representative sites across the country between 2006 and 2008. Seen through her lens here, the cemeteries are a rich amalgam of animist motifs, Islamic and Sufic iconography, and bear the distinct traces of Kyrgyzstan’s Soviet past.”.

The Farley

The Farley is a portrait of the 1.4 million square-foot Farley Post Office before a portion of it was gutted and repurposed as Moynihan Train Station, first proposed by Senator Daniel Patrick Moynihan in 1993. Built in 1914, the Post Office on Eighth Avenue between 31st and 32nd Streets was designed by McKim, Mead, and White, the same architects who built the original Pennsylvania Station, demolished in 1963. Two years after New York State purchased the building from the USPS in 2007, the Farley ceased its 24-hour postal operations for the first time in nearly a century, choosing to operate out of other sites around New York City.
